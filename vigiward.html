VigiWard

Pharmacovigilance from ordinary pharmacy, laboratory and mortality data — finding the suspected adverse reactions nobody reported, ranking the patients most worth a chart review, and turning confirmed cases into TMDA reports. Named for where the gap is biggest: pharmacovigilance in the wards.

Open the live demo Request VigiWard

Why this exists

Formal adverse-drug-reaction reporting depends on someone noticing a reaction and filling out a form — and reporting rates in this setting run at roughly one report per million population per year, several orders of magnitude below what's actually happening. VigiWard works the other direction: it looks for prescribing patterns in data hospitals already generate — pharmacy dispensing sequences, lab result timing — that suggest a reaction occurred even when nobody filed a report.

What it does

Sequence symmetry analysis

For drug pairs where one commonly follows the other as a treatment for a side effect (the classic example: a laxative prescribed shortly after starting an opioid), the tool counts how often the sequence runs forward versus backward and flags pairs where that asymmetry is statistically unlikely to be chance — with a proper Wilson-derived confidence interval, not a raw ratio.

Lab-anchored signal detection

Cross-references pharmacy dispensing against lab result timing and mortality data to surface concerning temporal associations — always labeled as hypothesis-generating, never as a causal finding, since that determination needs clinical adjudication VigiWard doesn't replace.

Watch-list & ward huddle

Instead of thousands of dispensing lines, the team starts from a ranked list of the patients most likely to have had a reaction — and a printable ward huddle sheet (English or Kiswahili) of who is on a high-risk medicine today and what to watch for.

Spike alerts

A sudden rise in reactions to one medicine in one month, tested against the rest of the data with an exact Poisson test adjusted for department mix — the pattern a bad batch, a new supplier or a substandard product would leave.

NCD audit & aDSM

From the same dispensing feed: a cardiometabolic prescribing audit against WHO HEARTS, KDIGO and the national guideline, and active drug-safety monitoring for TB and HIV medicines — were the scheduled liver, blood-count and kidney tests actually done?

Case review in REDCap: how often is each rule right?

A chosen set of flags, for example a random sample from each detection method, goes to REDCap as an ADR case-review form. Pharmacists record on chart review whether each flag was a real reaction, the WHO-UMC causality, the ten Naranjo questions, seriousness, outcome and the TMDA report. Brought back, the reviews give the share of each method's flags that were real reactions (positive predictive value) with a 95% interval: the evidence a committee needs before it trusts a rule.

TMDA-aligned draft reports

Flagged signals can be exported into a draft aligned with TMDA's yellow-form pharmacovigilance reporting structure — a head start for the person who still has to review the chart and make the clinical call, not a replacement for them.

Showcase (3.15)

A stage for a talk (★ Showcase in the top bar, in the live demo too). VigiWard opens empty; the sample hospital's exports go in one by one, dispensing, laboratory results, the deaths register, NCD readings and the pharmacist reviews, the counts rise on an assembly line, and a ledger shows that rows in = rows used + rows set aside. Each headline figure starts red, naming the file it still needs, and turns green when that file arrives. Then Reports, one click writes real files from the data just loaded: the TMDA ADR report and the printable Yellow Form, ICH E2B(R3) case reports, the WHO VigiFlow worksheet, the REDCap case review, the Stata data file, the signal line list and the facility PV report. It ends on decisions for the PV team and for management. Keys: → next, ← back, 1–8 the outputs, F full screen, Esc opens the tool; a presentation clicker works. It runs fully offline with Odieman_Sample_Hospital.js beside the tool, and in a full copy it can also run on the data loaded now (your own, nothing saved) or on files dragged onto the stage.

Pharmacology check (3.15)

On every flag, beside the card and on its own button: what the medicine is known to do (its labelled reactions, the Rawlins type, the mechanism), who is at higher risk of it, and what this patient's own record shows: age, sex, ward, and the creatinine with an eGFR (CKD-EPI 2021), potassium, ALT, glucose and neutrophils measured around the event. It ends in a plausibility reading for the reviewer. It is not a causality score: Naranjo and WHO-UMC remain the causality tools. Profiles are built in for about 71 medicines in common use, each reviewed against its sources: the WHO Model Formulary, the BNF, the medicines' SmPCs, KDIGO, the Tanzania STG/NEMLIT 2021 and the WHO TB, HIV and malaria guidelines.

Built-in caution

Every signal VigiWard surfaces carries the same three qualifiers, deliberately, every time:

QualifierWhat it means in practice
Crude and trend-adjustedThe crude sequence ratio is shown next to a null-effect (trend-adjusted) ratio, which corrects for drugs simply becoming more or less common over the period
Minimum-power gatedA signal only fires with ≥ 5 discordant pairs and a lower CI bound above 1 — sparse data doesn't get to trigger a flag
Hypothesis-generatingPoints a reviewer at a chart worth pulling — it is not itself a diagnosis or a causality determination
