A worked example · about 10 minutes

Thirty-three months in the life of a hospital’s antibiotics

Demo Regional Referral Hospital does not exist. Its admissions, dispensing records, laboratory results and ward surveys were simulated, and a stewardship programme that slowly gets better was written into them. What is real is everything below: every figure was produced by AMS Pro, PPS Pro and VigiWard reading those files, exactly as they would read yours.

Read this first. Because the improvement was written into the data, this page cannot show that stewardship works. It shows what the tools see when it does — and, twice, a number you should not believe. Each chapter ends with a link that opens the same figure in the live demo, so you can check it.

Chapter 1

Less antibiotic, measured the standard way

A day of therapy is one patient receiving one antibiotic for one day. Dividing by patient-days lets a busy quarter be compared fairly with a quiet one. AMS Pro reads the days from the dosage text that GoTHoMIS already writes on every dispensing line (“Duration: 5 /7”), so nobody has to count anything by hand.

The last point, Q3 2026, covers July to September; the exports end on 30 September 2026.

Chapter 2

From Watch to Access

In 2026 the hospital reached the WHO target of 60% Access for the first time, and two independent sources agree.

The WHO AWaRe classification asks that at least 60% of antibiotic use comes from the Access group: first-choice medicines such as amoxicillin, metronidazole and gentamicin. Watch antibiotics like ceftriaxone have a place, but a smaller one.

The blue line comes from dispensing records, every line classified and weighted by its defined daily doses. The ochre points come from something else entirely: five ward surveys, each a single day on which every inpatient’s treatment chart was read. They measure in different ways, so they never match exactly. What matters is that they move together. When two measurements made in different ways tell the same story, an AMS committee can trust it.

Chapter 3

Surgical prophylaxis: one dose, not five days

A single dose before the incision protects a surgical wound as well as a week of cover, and the extra days only add side-effects and resistance. AMS Pro joins the procedures report to the dispensing records and lists every patient still receiving antibiotics two days after an operation. PPS Pro asks the same question at the bedside on survey day.

This chapter found a bug. While building it, an operation recorded in both the procedures report and the admissions register was being counted twice, so every review prompt appeared twice. AMS Pro now counts each operation once (fixed in October 2026). A story that is checked number by number keeps the tools honest too.

Chapter 4

Injections that could be tablets

Ward pharmacies issue injectables one day at a time, and GoTHoMIS records each day as its own line. AMS Pro strings those days together. Three days of the same injection is a prompt to ask a simple question on the ward round: can this patient now take tablets? Switching saves money and nursing time and removes a drip that can itself become infected.

A prompt is not an error. Dispensing records cannot see fever, vomiting, sepsis or meningitis, which is why the Time-out list asks for review rather than giving a verdict, and why this line will never reach zero.

Chapter 5

Evidence before prescribing

Better prescribing is mostly better information. A culture result only changes treatment if a sample was taken before the first dose and the result reaches the clinician while it still matters. The surveys show the habits forming: a reason written in the notes, a date set to stop or review, a sample sent to the laboratory. The dispensing and laboratory exports show the same change from the other side, with more cultures sent and more results reported within the target time.

Chapter 6

Resistance: the chapter where nothing happens

Resistance did not clearly change in 33 months, and the tools say why you should not expect it to.

Percentage of isolates resistant to ceftriaxone, first isolate per patient per organism each year. * Fewer than 30 isolates: CLSI M39 advises against reporting a percentage on its own, and AMS Pro marks these cells.

Resistance moves over years, not quarters, and a regional hospital grows few isolates. So the honest summary is not a trend but a description: in this hospital about half to two thirds of E. coli and Klebsiella resist ceftriaxone, which is exactly why ceftriaxone should not be the automatic first choice, while carbapenems still work against almost every E. coli. That is worth protecting, and it is the reason chapters 1 to 4 matter.
