Tanga Regional Referral Hospital · Afya eHMS · AMS committee

The data was already there. What was missing was a way to use it.

These tools were not planned as software. They grew out of the everyday work of a hospital antimicrobial stewardship committee, and out of one question that kept coming back: why does a report take days when the hospital system already holds the answers?

The committee's problem

For about three years I served as assistant secretary of the Antimicrobial Stewardship team and committee at Tanga Regional Referral Hospital (Bombo), where I worked as a pharmaceutical technician for nine years. Much of the committee's data work landed on my desk.

An AMS committee is asked to do a lot with very little. We ran point prevalence surveys, monitored antibiotic use and watched for adverse drug reactions, mostly on paper forms and spreadsheets that took days to clean and analyse. By the time a report reached the committee, the data was often old. Reporting of adverse reactions from the wards stayed low, because busy ward staff did not see it as part of their daily work.

Where the answers were sitting

The hospital system, Afya eHMS, was already recording almost everything the committee needed: every dispensing line, every admission, every laboratory and culture result. Nobody had to collect new data. What was missing was a tool that could take those raw exports, put them together, and turn them into the indicators and reports a committee actually reads.

So I started building one. I downloaded the raw exports from Afya eHMS, fed them into the first versions of the tools, and checked the reports and slides that came out against what we knew from the wards.

Three tools, one data source

PPS Pro — point prevalence surveys

Built so that the moment data collection ends, the full WHO-method PPS report is ready with one click — while the findings are still fresh enough to act on.

AMS Pro — antibiotic use and resistance

Consumption by AWaRe group, DDD and DOT trends, Pareto analysis, costs and improvement cycles, built from the pharmacy export.

VigiWard — pharmacovigilance in the wards

The name means exactly that. Ward-level pharmacovigilance is the main gap across Tanzania: the wards are where most reactions happen and where few are reported. VigiWard looks for possible reaction signals in the routine pharmacy and lab data, so the wards do not have to fill in extra forms first.

All three read the same exports, and each one uses only what it needs. Everything runs on the user's own computer, with no internet and no server; the outputs are aggregate figures, not patient names.

The figures shown on this website and in the live demos come from a simulated demonstration hospital, never from a real facility's records.

Where it stands today

The tools are in routine use by the antimicrobial stewardship and pharmacovigilance teams at Tanga Regional Referral Hospital, on ordinary hospital computers.

I have since moved to Matema Health Center in Kyela District, a new facility, and I keep developing the tools from there. This work was done together with Dr. Zalha Nuhu, Chairperson of the Antimicrobial Stewardship Committee at Tanga RRH, who has supported it from the committee side from the start.

Why this page exists

The tools were built by one person, as a self-taught builder working alongside a full-time pharmacy job. The aim is for the idea to outlive any single builder: a hospital system already collects the data, and a small offline tool can turn it into stewardship.

If you run an AMS or pharmacovigilance programme, work with TMDA, maintain hospital systems, or study antimicrobial resistance, I would like to hear from you — to pilot the tools on your own data, test them, or help carry them forward.
