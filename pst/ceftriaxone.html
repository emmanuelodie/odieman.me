A special ceftriaxone stewardship for Tanzania

Ceftriaxone carries our wards. Cotrimoxazole was once cheap, everywhere and first-line; today it fails for most urine infections. If we keep using ceftriaxone the way we used cotrimoxazole, in ten years we will say the same about it. This page sets out the proposal in short, then the studies behind it.

The proposal in short

It covers every Watch antibiotic, and ceftriaxone above all.

  1. Every Watch antibiotic only on a prescription from a health facility. All antibiotics are already prescription-only by law; the Watch group is where enforcement should start.
  2. Ceftriaxone used in hospitals and health centres only. Supply to ADDOs and other retail outlets is cut. A community pharmacy may dispense it only against a prescription from a registered hospital or health centre.
  3. Any sale outside a facility tracked, anonymously: the prescribing facility’s registration code, the date and the quantity. No patient name.
  4. Inside facilities, started on signs of a bacterial infection, with a culture sent where one can be, and reviewed at 48–72 hours: stop, narrow, or switch to oral.
  5. Surgical prophylaxis (SP1): cefazolin, one dose before the incision, as the 2026 STG now allows. Not ceftriaxone, and not for days.
  6. The exceptions the STG already allows stay: a single dose for gonorrhoea, and a pre-referral dose for a very sick patient, recorded the same way.

This is a proposal for PST, the Ministry of Health, TMDA, the Pharmacy Council and MSD to shape together. It is not current law, and it is written to start that discussion.

1. Ceftriaxone already carries our hospitals

Point prevalence surveys in Tanzanian hospitals, using the WHO method or close to it.

StudySettingWhat it found
Katyali et al., 2023 [1]Muhimbili National Hospital, 202247% of inpatients on antibiotics, every prescription empirical, guideline compliance 45%. Citing earlier national surveys: ceftriaxone is the most prescribed Watch antibiotic in zonal, regional and district hospitals.
Seni et al., 2020 [2]Six referral hospitals, December 201962.3% of inpatients on antibiotics, mostly ceftriaxone, metronidazole or ampicillin–cloxacillin. The authors called for urgent action on the lack of culture and susceptibility testing.
Horumpende et al., 2020 [3]KCMC, Mawenzi and St. Joseph, KilimanjaroCeftriaxone was the most prescribed antibiotic: 29.8% of all prescriptions.
Mutatina et al., 2025 [4]Tanga Regional Referral Hospital, April 2023Metronidazole (27.7%) and ceftriaxone (17.6%) were the most prescribed antibiotics. Only 2.4% of prescriptions were backed by susceptibility testing.

Two things stand out. Ceftriaxone is the default broad antibiotic of our wards, and it is almost always given without a culture result, so nobody knows whether it was the right choice.

2. Surgical prophylaxis is where much of it goes

StudySettingWhat it found
Sonda et al., 2019 [5]KCMC, 2013–2015Half of all inpatients (51.1%, 322 of 630) were on ceftriaxone during their stay, and 44 of 108 surgical patients (40.7%) got it as surgical prophylaxis. The authors warn that if ceftriaxone resistance spreads, few alternatives exist and most patients cannot afford them.
Horumpende et al., 2020 [3]Three Kilimanjaro hospitalsProphylaxis was continued for more than 3 days in 57% of cases.
Nyongole et al., 2015 [6]Muhimbili, urology, six yearsProphylactic antibiotics were often continued beyond five days; ceftriaxone was the most given prophylactic antibiotic whatever the operation (as summarised in [5]).
Dodoma RRH, 2020 [7]Caesarean sectionsBefore the programme, the usual course was 3 days of IV ceftriaxone with metronidazole, then oral antibiotics. A joint infection-prevention and stewardship programme, which moved prophylaxis to before the incision (98% of patients, against 2% before), cut surgical site infections from 48% to 17%.
Bugando, 2013 [8]Emergency caesarean sections, randomised trialThe authors recommend a single pre-operative dose of prophylaxis for emergency caesarean section.

Prophylaxis is meant to be one dose before the incision. In our hospitals it is often ceftriaxone, and often for days. That is the single easiest place to save ceftriaxone without risking a patient.

3. Why cefazolin for SP1, and not ampicillin

Many facilities moved to ceftriaxone for prophylaxis because the older choice no longer looks safe. The data agree that ampicillin has largely gone:

StudySettingAmpicillinCotrimoxazole
Silago et al., 2024 [9]Urine Gram-negatives, five Mwanza hospitals, 202311% still susceptible27% still susceptible
Schmider et al., 2022 [10]Community urine infections, Bagamoyo74% of E. coli resistant76% of E. coli resistant
Morogoro outpatients, 2023 [11]Urine isolatesAll isolated bacteria more than 85% resistant to both

The answer is not to replace ampicillin with ceftriaxone. WHO’s Essential Medicines List names cefazolin, alone or with metronidazole, as the first choice for surgical prophylaxis, an Access antibiotic; amoxicillin–clavulanic acid and gentamicin are second choices [12]. Tanzania’s STG/NEMLIT 7th Edition, 2026 now lists cefazolin as an Access antibiotic for surgical prophylaxis [13]. One dose of cefazolin before the incision protects the patient and leaves ceftriaxone for the infections that need it.

4. The cotrimoxazole warning

Cotrimoxazole was cheap, sold almost everywhere and widely prescribed. Look at it now:

StudySettingResistance to cotrimoxazole
Silago et al., 2024 [9]Mwanza, urine Gram-negativesOnly 27% still susceptible
Schmider et al., 2022 [10]Bagamoyo, E. coli76% resistant
Muhimbili, 2010 [14]ESBL-producing urinary E. coli90.7% resistant
Ifakara, 2025 [15]ESBL-producing E. coli, urine100% resistant

Ceftriaxone is already sliding the same way. In Mwanza, the share of Klebsiella from blood resistant to cefotaxime, the same class as ceftriaxone, rose from 65% in 2019–20 to 93% in 2023 [16]. Across the WHO African Region, resistance of E. coli and Klebsiella to third-generation cephalosporins now exceeds 70% [17]. And use breeds resistance: recent exposure to third-generation cephalosporins was linked to more than three times the odds of carrying ESBL-producing bacteria in one study of children [18].

Cotrimoxazole was lost slowly, through cheap and easy use. Ceftriaxone is following the same road, faster, and there is no cheap antibiotic behind it.

5. Why the supply outside facilities matters

About 80% of the world’s antibiotics are used outside hospitals [19]. In Tanzania, the law already makes every antibiotic prescription-only, yet:

  • Mystery clients bought amoxicillin without a prescription in 88.2% of community pharmacies and ADDOs in Mwanza, Mbeya and Kilimanjaro, and most outlets sold a half course [20].
  • For urine symptoms with no prescription, sellers recommended antibiotics in about nine visits in ten, most often azithromycin or ciprofloxacin, both Watch [19].
  • In ADDOs, 85% of dispensers sold an antibiotic on request [21]. ADDOs are allowed only a limited list of antibiotics, and only with a prescription [22].

A rule on paper has not been enough. That is why this proposal starts with the supply itself, then adds a record that can be checked.

6. How it could work

  1. Cut the supply where it should not be. MSD and licensed wholesalers supply ceftriaxone to registered hospitals and health centres, and to community pharmacies only in amounts that match facility prescriptions they have filled. ADDOs and other retail outlets do not stock it.
  2. Track it anonymously outside facilities. Each sale by a community pharmacy records the prescribing facility’s registration code, the date and the quantity, with no patient name. A PST-held tool in every community pharmacy could do this offline and sync when it can (the idea on the PST page). The national picture then shows, for the first time, where ceftriaxone leaves the facilities.
  3. Steward it inside facilities. Start on signs of a bacterial infection, send a culture where possible, review at 48–72 hours, and use cefazolin, one dose, for surgical prophylaxis.
  4. Measure it, every month. AMS Pro already reports ceftriaxone DDD per 100 bed-days, ward by ward, from routine pharmacy exports. With the new OPD vs Inpatients split, outpatient ceftriaxone is counted apart from inpatient use, so a falling inpatient figure is not hidden by clinic dispensing, or the reverse.
  5. Keep the exceptions honest. The single dose for gonorrhoea and a pre-referral dose stay available, and are recorded like every other sale.

7. Has anyone tried this?

India created a separate class, Schedule H1, in force since March 2014. It covers third- and fourth-generation cephalosporins and carbapenems, among others. Each sale must be written in a separate register with the patient’s identity, the prescriber and the quantity, kept for three years, and the pack carries a red Rx mark [23]. An analysis of private-sector sales found that use of H1 antimicrobials still rose between 2008 and 2018 [24]. The lesson for us: a register on its own is not enough. Supply control and data that someone actually reads must come with it. That is the difference this proposal tries to make.

8. Honest limits

  • It needs the regulators, the Ministry, MSD and PST around one table. No tool can do this alone.
  • Health centres must keep enough ceftriaxone for the patients who truly need it. Restricting supply must never create a stock-out where it is the right drug.
  • Cefazolin must actually be on the shelves before ceftriaxone is taken out of prophylaxis.
  • Anonymous tracking needs a facility code on every prescription and a way to check it, and must follow Tanzania’s data protection rules.
  • The resistance figures come from different studies, places and years. They point the same way, but they are not one national survey.
