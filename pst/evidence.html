From the world to our wards: the evidence, and where the tools stand

What the studies say about antibiotic use, resistance and medicine safety, first for the world, then for Africa, then for Tanzania. How those trends meet what the three tools were built to do. How the tools stand beside the systems the world already uses, where those systems are stronger, and why ours still fit where the global prescriptions do not. And why the statistics inside the tools are the ones they are.

In short The world Africa Tanzania Where the trends meet the tools Beside the world’s tools Why ours still fit Statistics At scale Honest limits

In short

  1. Resistance is rising, and it hits hardest where health systems are weakest. In 2023, one in six laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections worldwide was resistant to treatment, and one in five in the WHO African Region [3].
  2. Africa measures least where it suffers most. Across 14 African countries, only 1.3% of medical laboratories do any bacteriology [6], and the continent sends under 1% of the world’s adverse drug reaction reports [9].
  3. Tanzanian hospital surveys tell one story. Roughly half to two-thirds of inpatients are on antibiotics, ceftriaxone leads, and almost none of it is guided by a culture result [12–15].
  4. The data is collected but rarely analysed. Hospitals now run electronic systems, yet surveys are done by hand and rarely repeated, and adverse reactions wait for someone to report them. That gap is exactly what the three tools were built for.
  5. The world’s tools are good, and ours are built to feed them, not replace them. Where the global prescriptions assume a connected health record, a microbiology laboratory, steady internet or very large numbers, these tools work with what a Tanzanian hospital actually has.

Our advice, honestly

  • If your laboratory runs susceptibility testing, keep WHONET in the laboratory. AMS Pro will read its results or send them on.
  • Take part in Global-PPS rounds when you can, for the comparison with the world. Use PPS Pro to survey more often in between.
  • Send every confirmed reaction to TMDA. VigiWard helps you find them and fill the form; the national system is where they count.
  • If a hospital has a full electronic record with medication administration and live interfaces, real-time stewardship software can do things these tools cannot, and it should be considered.

6. Statistics: what was chosen, what was left out, and why

Each method answers a question a hospital actually asks, with the numbers one hospital has. A test that answers a different question adds p-values, not decisions, so it was left out on purpose. The formulas are on the methodology page.

ChosenThe question it answersWhere
Wilson 95% intervalsHow sure is this percentage? It stays honest with small denominators and near 0% or 100%, where the common ± interval breaks down.All three tools
Laney p′ control charts with run rules [28]Did something change, or is this an ordinary month? A point outside the limits, 8 in a row on one side, or 6 rising or falling.AMS Pro
Interrupted time series (segmented regression) [29]Did an intervention change the level or the trend, beyond the trend already under way? Standard errors allow for months that follow each other, and that is checked. It is one of the study designs Cochrane’s EPOC group accepts when a trial is not possible [30].AMS Pro
Chi-square or Fisher’s exact; Welch t; Mann–WhitneyFor a planned before-and-after comparison on a ward project, with patients as the units.AMS Pro ward projects
Trend-adjusted sequence symmetry analysis [27]Is drug B started after drug A more often than chance and changing prescribing habits explain? It needs only the dispensing record, and flags only when the lower bound clears 1 with at least 5 pairs.VigiWard
Exact Poisson test, adjusted for department mixIs this month’s rise in reactions to one medicine a possible bad batch? p < 0.01 is recommended, with a minimum number of patients.VigiWard
Naranjo and WHO-UMC causality; reviewer-confirmed positive predictive valueIs this patient’s event a reaction to this medicine, and how often are the flags right? A judgement by a person, not a test.VigiWard

Left out on purpose

  • Disproportionality statistics (the reporting odds ratio and similar). They are built for national and global databases with thousands of reports [31]. At one hospital, a handful of reports would make them flag chance. They belong at TMDA and in VigiBase.
  • Testing every ward, sex or age group against every other with chi-square or ANOVA. With twenty comparisons at p < 0.05, about one comes out “significant” by chance, and differences between groups mostly reflect who the patients are, not how they were treated.
  • Correlating a dose with a laboratory value. Doctors lower the dose when the kidneys are failing, so the line can run backwards and mislead a causality assessment.
  • Group averages to judge one patient. A before-and-after mean across many patients cannot say whether this patient had a reaction, and that is the question pharmacovigilance asks.
  • Forecasts. Where a chart continues a line, it is labelled as a continuation of the recent past, a what-if for a budget conversation, never a prediction.

What we gain by these choices

Fewer false alarms. Every figure carries its uncertainty. A committee gets results it can act on, in methods a reviewer recognises. And the tools say plainly when a change cannot be claimed, for example when the fall was already under way before the intervention.

What can still be noise

  • Small wards. With fifteen patients a month, one month can swing widely. The intervals show it, but the eye still reads the point.
  • A change in the data, not the practice. A new export layout, a renamed ward or a change of supplier can look like a change in prescribing. AMS Pro shows the volume and department mix beside its charts for this reason.
  • Many charts at once. Look at thirty indicators every month and one will cross a limit by chance. Trust signals that persist, or that you planned to look for.
  • What the record is. A dispensing line is not a dose given, and a flag is not a reaction until a person has reviewed it.

7. The future we want: many facilities, then a multisite pilot

With many facilities, methods that are wrong for one hospital become right, and stronger designs become possible.

  1. Validation first. Compare the tools with the usual method in three or four hospitals: a manual survey beside PPS Pro, chart review beside VigiWard’s flags, reported for sensitivity and positive predictive value.
  2. One language for the data. The same data dictionary, the same DDD per 100 bed-days and the same AWaRe version everywhere, so a figure from Tanga means the same as a figure from Mbeya.
  3. Fair comparison between facilities. Funnel plots, whose limits widen for small facilities so they are not shamed by chance [32], and multilevel models that separate real differences between facilities from noise.
  4. A stepped-wedge pilot. Facilities start using the tools at different, planned times, so each serves as its own comparison and the whole pilot gives far stronger evidence than a before-and-after at one site [33].
  5. Signal detection where it belongs. Confirmed reactions pooled nationally through TMDA, where disproportionality becomes meaningful [31]; the tools already write the E2B files that make pooling possible.
  6. Count models for small facilities. Health centres with few patients a month need Poisson or negative binomial models with bed-days as the denominator, not straight lines.
  7. Analysis plans written before the data is seen, so nobody, including me, can go looking for a result.

Further on: if the methods ran inside GoTHoMIS

Reading GoTHoMIS exports is the first step. The bigger one would be for the methods in these tools to run inside GoTHoMIS itself, the system PO-RALG (TAMISEMI) runs in district hospitals, health centres and dispensaries, which has already taken on new modules such as immunization [34]. If that happened, this is what could be expected:

  • No exports, no typing. The reports would come from what the facility already enters, every month, without anyone downloading a file.
  • Every facility, not one. Each district hospital and health centre would see its own antibiotic use, AWaRe shares and a list of patients to review for reactions; the council, region and nation would see the same figures added up and compared fairly.
  • From looking back to acting in time. A check could run at the moment of prescribing or dispensing: a Watch antibiotic with no indication, a course running past its days, a rescue medicine that suggests a reaction. Today the tools can only look back.
  • Reports that travel on their own. Confirmed reactions would reach TMDA electronically, and facility antibiotic use would roll up for national and WHO GLASS reporting, instead of being typed again.
  • Evidence at national scale. With every facility on the same methods, a stepped-wedge rollout across councils could show whether stewardship works, and signal detection would at last have the numbers it needs.

What it would take: a decision by PO-RALG and the Ministry of Health, not by me; methods that have passed validation first; only aggregate figures leaving the facility, as the Personal Data Protection Act expects; and few, well-chosen alerts, or staff will learn to ignore them. A system can produce the list. People still have to act on it.

None of this needs to be invented. It needs facilities willing to take part, an institution to hold the tools, and partners to evaluate them. The Forum 5 answers say more about what scaling would take.

8. Honest limits

  • The tools have not been formally validated yet. Every release is checked figure by figure against the one before, but a proper validation in several hospitals is still to come.
  • They see only what a hospital exports. A poor export gives a poor analysis, and the tools can only warn about what they can detect.
  • They were built around Afya eHMS, the system of the referral hospitals. GoTHoMIS, which runs in district hospitals, health centres and dispensaries [18], has not been tested yet. That is my focus now: I work at a health centre that uses GoTHoMIS, I am studying its exports, and the aim is for all three tools to read them by the end of February 2027.
  • They are retrospective. They do not alert at the bedside in real time.
  • They are built and maintained by one person today. That is their weakest point, and the reason for looking for an institutional home.
  • The studies on this page come from different years, places and methods. They point the same way, but they are not one national survey.
  • The descriptions of other systems are from their public material as of November 2026 and may have changed.
